# Product Roadmap Software vs Spreadsheets vs Building Your Own: How to Choose

Author: Lovable Team at Lovable

## TL;DR

- **A spreadsheet is genuinely fine** if you are a solo PM or a two-person startup with one team, a simple timeline, and few stakeholders to keep aligned. Don't overbuy.
- **Dedicated roadmap software earns its per-maker price** when you have multiple stakeholder audiences and need feedback intake, prioritisation scoring, issue-tracker sync, and polished read-only views maintained for you.
- **The per-maker meter is the catch.** Plans commonly run roughly $19–$99 per maker per month, and the tier with the prioritisation and integration features you want is usually the expensive one.
- **Viewers are not always free.** The free-viewer-versus-paid-maker split varies by tool, and seat minimums plus block-of-five scaling quietly floor your bill.
- **There is a third path the listicles skip:** building a roadmap tool that mirrors your exact prioritisation logic and stakeholder views, with no per-maker fee.
- **Building used to be the expensive option.** A traditional internal tool runs $17,000–$22,000 and about eight weeks, but a focused first version is now a matter of hours, not a project.

Choosing product roadmap software starts with an honest question about your own team. You maintain a roadmap in a spreadsheet that two other people also edit, and none of you fully trusts what it says. An exec screenshots a date from last quarter and quotes it back to you as a commitment. An engineer builds off a "now" column that sales already contradicted to a customer, and you cannot produce one clean view for engineering, leadership, and customers without rebuilding it three times by hand. This is the stale-roadmap moment, and it costs you alignment, not because you are disorganised, but because the tool cannot hold your process.

That pain has two well-marketed answers and one that almost nobody tells you about. You can keep the spreadsheet. You can buy per-maker roadmap software, which commonly runs roughly $19–$99 per user per month once you are on a tier that does prioritisation scoring and integrations. Or you can build a roadmap tool that fits how your team prioritises. Most content on this keyword is a vendor-owned or affiliate listicle that ranks a dozen tools and resolves to "pick one of these," so the honest three-way decision rarely gets written down. Which option is right depends on how you score, sequence, and share your work.

## This Is a Three-Way Decision, Not a Binary

The choice usually gets framed as spreadsheet versus software. That framing hides the option that fits a large group of readers best. Before comparing features, place yourself honestly in one of three buckets.

**You're a spreadsheet user** if you run one team, a simple timeline, and a short stakeholder list. A solo PM or a two-person startup with a quarterly plan does not need a subscription or a build. A clean sheet and a shared link win here, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. The same honest starting point applies to sales pipelines, which the [CRM vs spreadsheet guide](https://lovable.dev/guides/crm-vs-spreadsheet-for-sales-pipeline) works through with the same three-way logic.

**You're a roadmap-software buyer** if you serve multiple stakeholder audiences and need the full stack working today: structured feedback and idea intake, a built-in prioritisation framework, two-way issue-tracker sync, effort and capacity fields, and polished read-only or comment views for execs and customers. For a busy product org, a mature platform earns its per-maker price. That price buys you not having to build or maintain any of it.

**You're a build-your-own candidate** if you sit in the gap between those two. Your prioritisation process is defined and repeatable, you know exactly what views each stakeholder group needs, and off-the-shelf tools almost fit but force you to bend your scoring model to their schema. You resent paying per maker for a shape that is not yours. That gap is where a built tool wins, and until recently it was the option nobody could reach.

Two things stand out. The capabilities that separate dedicated software from a spreadsheet, meaning structured feedback intake, two-way issue-tracker sync, and role-based stakeholder access, sit behind paid tiers and per-maker seats. And the one column a spreadsheet wins outright is fit to your exact prioritisation logic, because a blank grid imposes no schema on you. That last column is the whole argument for building your own, minus the manual maintenance a spreadsheet demands.

The read-only sharing row deserves a note, because it is the seat-cost lever most teams misjudge. The common assumption is that viewers are always free. The reality varies: some tools give you free contributors who can comment, some make read-only reviewers free only on their top enterprise tiers, some cap included reviewers and then charge per head above the cap, and some bundle unlimited viewers on every paid plan. Confirm the exact split before you size a plan, because it decides whether sharing with 30 execs and customers is free or a line item.

## The Real Cost Is the Per-Maker Math, Not the Sticker Price

A spreadsheet looks free, and for a small team it effectively is. The hidden cost is the maintenance labour in every manual update and the risk of a decision made off a stale plan. Spreadsheets are also unreliable at scale in a documented way. Field audits reviewed by [Raymond Panko](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0802.3457) found errors in 94% of the spreadsheets examined in the most rigorous of those audits, and while that research measured calculation errors rather than roadmap-date drift, the lesson transfers: a hand-maintained grid that three people edit will contradict itself.

Dedicated roadmap software makes the cost visible and turns it into a bill that scales with your maker count. Entry tiers look harmless, and the features you want live higher up. Per the vendors' public 2026 pricing pages, the range across the category runs from about $9 per seat per month at the low end to $149 per user per month at the enterprise end:

- Productboard prices makers at $19 per maker per month on Plus and $59 per maker per month on Business, which carries a two-maker minimum; contributors who give feedback are free.
- Roadmunk (Strategic Roadmaps by Tempo) prices editors at $19, $49, and $99 per editor per month across three tiers, with a capped number of reviewers included, then $5 per month for each additional reviewer.
- ProductPlan runs $39 and $69 per editor per month with unlimited viewers included.
- Aha! Roadmaps prices per user at $59 on Premium and $99 on Enterprise on annual billing (Premium is $74.59 billed monthly), with free read-only reviewers only on the enterprise tiers, not on Premium; its top Enterprise+ plan is $149 per user per month.
- airfocus lists only "Request pricing" on its public page, so treat it as quote-based rather than a fixed number.
- Adjacent work-management and database tools sit near the category: monday prices seats at $9, $12, and $19 per seat per month annually, with a three-seat minimum that then scales in blocks of five; Airtable prices at $20 and $45 per user per month with free read-only collaborators; and Jira Product Discovery prices creators at $10 and $25 per creator per month, with free contributors and up to three creators on its free tier.

Model your real team, not the entry price. Five makers on a $59-per-maker plan is about $3,540 a year before any add-ons, and that is only the maker seats. Once you factor the two-maker minimums, the three-seat floors, and the block-of-five scaling, the bill floors and then steps up in chunks whether or not you fill every seat.

To ground the alternative, a comparable internal tool built through traditional development costs between $17,000 and $22,000 in direct developer labour and takes roughly eight weeks to ship, per a Forrester Total Economic Impact study of Microsoft Power Pages. That figure is developer time only, with no design, no QA, and no maintenance. For years that number was the reason not to build, and it is why "just subscribe" was sound advice. The broader picture in the [2026 app cost guide](https://lovable.dev/guides/how-much-does-it-cost-to-make-an-app) shows why that reason no longer holds.

A large product org that needs feedback intake, shipped prioritisation frameworks, two-way integrations, and stakeholder views maintained for you: the per-maker software earns its price, and buying it is the right call, not a compromise. None of this makes dedicated roadmap software a villain, because for that reader it removes real work from your plate.

## Switching Is About Adoption, Not Migration

Whichever direction you move, the hard part is not the data. Moving off a spreadsheet or off a platform means porting your items and their history, mapping your prioritisation scores and custom fields, wiring the issue-tracker sync, and then getting stakeholders to open the new view. That last step is where most rollouts stall. A roadmap the team quietly ignores is worse than the spreadsheet you already had.

Off-the-shelf software carries an adoption tax that rarely gets discussed: you reshape your prioritisation process to fit the vendor's schema, and you feel that friction on every re-score. Building your own inverts the problem. The tool matches how your team already scores and sequences work, so there is less to unlearn and less reason to route around it. A roadmap hub is a textbook internal tool, and [how to build an internal tool without code](https://lovable.dev/guides/how-to-build-an-internal-tool-without-code) covers the same pattern end to end.

Start with a first version that covers your most common view and your current prioritisation formula, get a few PMs and one exec using it on the real backlog, and iterate from what they hit. You are not trying to replicate an enterprise platform on day one. You are building the roadmap your team will trust, and growing it from there.

## FAQ

### What Is the Best Product Roadmap Software for a Small Team?

For a solo PM or a two-person team with a simple quarterly plan, the best product roadmap software is often a spreadsheet or a free tier, because you do not need feedback intake, prioritisation frameworks, or role-based stakeholder views yet. Once you have multiple stakeholder audiences and a real prioritisation process, either a dedicated platform or a tool you build yourself fits better. Choose by whether off-the-shelf scoring matches how you prioritise, not by feature count.

### Do I Need Dedicated Roadmap Software, or Is a Spreadsheet Enough?

A spreadsheet is enough when one team maintains a simple timeline and few stakeholders need to see it. You have outgrown it when three people edit a plan nobody trusts, when you cannot produce a clean read-only view for execs and customers, or when your prioritisation scoring lives in fragile hand-maintained formulas. At that point you either buy dedicated software or build a tool that holds your process.

### How Much Does Product Roadmap Software Cost per User?

Public 2026 pricing across the category runs roughly $9–$149 per user per month, with most maker or editor seats landing around $19–$99 depending on tier. The catch is that prioritisation frameworks, integrations, and role-based sharing usually sit in the higher tiers, and seat minimums plus block scaling floor your bill. Model the annual total for your real maker count, and confirm whether viewers are free on the tier you need, because that varies by tool.

### Can I Build My Own Product Roadmap Tool Without Engineering?

Yes, and it is no longer a five-figure, multi-month project. ### What Is the Difference Between a Roadmap Tool and a Project Management Tool?

A project-management tool tracks tasks, assignees, and due dates for work already committed. A roadmap tool sits earlier: it captures feedback, scores and sequences ideas against a prioritisation framework, and shows the now/next/later plan to different stakeholder audiences. General work-management tools approximate roadmaps but do not specialise in prioritisation scoring or read-only stakeholder views, which is why teams outgrow them for either dedicated software or a build.

### How Do I Share a Product Roadmap With Stakeholders Without Paying for Extra Seats?

Check the viewer model on your tool, because it varies: some give free commenting contributors, some free viewers only on enterprise tiers, and some cap reviewers then charge per head. If read-only sharing keeps pushing you into a higher tier or per-seat add-ons, a tool you build lets you give unlimited stakeholders a view-only link with no per-seat meter, because you control view-versus-edit access yourself.
